Fever in Children: What Parents in New York and Everywhere Should Know

Fever is a common symptom in children and is usually caused by infections. This article provides evidence-based information about fever in children that applies to parents everywhere, including New York. For specific local guidance, always consult your pediatrician.

What Is a Fever?

A fever is a temporary rise in body temperature, often in response to an infection. It is the body's natural way of fighting off viruses or bacteria. In children, a rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher is generally considered a fever.

Common Causes of Fever in Children

Fever in children is most often caused by viral infections, such as the common cold, or bacterial infections. In babies and young children, fever can also be caused by ear infections, urinary tract infections, or other illnesses (Source: NIH | Common Infant and Newborn Problems: MedlinePlus – Fever (High Temperature) In Kids, Nemours Foundation).

When to Seek Medical Attention

Contact your child's health care provider if your child has a fever and any of these signs:

Age under 3 months: Any fever of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher in a baby under 3 months is a medical emergency—seek care immediately (Source: NIH | Common Infant and Newborn Problems: MedlinePlus – When to Seek Medical Attention, Mayo Foundation for Medical Education and Research).

Age 3–6 months: Fever of 102°F (38.9°C) or higher, or fever lasting more than 1 day.

Age 6–24 months: Fever lasting more than 1 day without other symptoms, or more than 3 days with mild symptoms.

Older children: Fever lasting more than 3 days without improvement.

Signs of dehydration: Fewer wet diapers, sunken eyes, dry mouth, no tears.

Difficulty breathing: Fast breathing, flaring nostrils, grunting, chest retractions.

Lethargy or difficulty waking: Unusually difficult to wake, floppy, or not interacting.

Seizure: Febrile seizures require emergency care.

Rash that does not fade when pressed (non-blanching).

Fever that returns after improving.

When Is Fever Normal?

Most fevers in children are harmless and resolve on their own within 2–3 days. If your child is otherwise comfortable, drinking well, and breathing normally, you can monitor at home. Treatment is meant for comfort, not to normalize the temperature.

Safe Home Care for Fever

Offer extra fluids (breast milk, formula, or water for older children) to prevent dehydration.

Dress in light clothing and keep the room comfortably cool—do not overdress.

Give fever-reducing medication (acetaminophen for children over 3 months, or ibuprofen for children over 6 months) only if your child is uncomfortable—dose strictly by weight.

Do not use cold baths or alcohol rubs—these can cause shivering and raise body temperature.

What to Avoid

Do not give aspirin to children with fever—it can cause Reye's syndrome.

Do not use over-the-counter cold and cough medicines in children under 6 years without a pediatrician's approval (Source: NIH | Common Infant and Newborn Problems: MedlinePlus – Common Cold in Babies: Symptoms and Causes, Mayo Foundation for Medical Education and Research).

Yellow Fever (For Travelers)

Yellow fever is a serious infection spread by mosquitoes (Source: NHS | Yellow fever – Yellow fever). It is not found in the United States, including New York (Source: NHS | Yellow fever – Yellow fever). However, if you are traveling to areas where yellow fever is found, such as parts of sub-Saharan Africa or South America, you should have a yellow fever vaccination and take precautions to avoid mosquito bites, including wearing long-sleeved clothing and trousers (Source: NHS | Yellow fever – Yellow fever). You can get health advice for a country you're travelling to on the TravelHealthPro website (Source: NHS | Yellow fever – Yellow fever).

The Bottom Line

Fever in children is common and usually harmless. Know the warning signs and when to seek medical attention. For travel-related concerns, such as yellow fever, consult a travel health specialist. Always trust your intuition—if you are worried about your child's health, contact your pediatrician.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: When should I call the doctor for my child's fever?

Call your pediatrician immediately if your child is under 3 months with any fever, has a fever lasting more than 3 days, signs of dehydration, difficulty breathing, lethargy, a seizure, or a non-blanching rash. If you are worried, contact your health care provider (Source: NIH | Common Infant and Newborn Problems: MedlinePlus – When to Seek Medical Attention, Mayo Foundation for Medical Education and Research).

Q: How long does a child's fever normally last?

Most viral fevers in children last 2 to 3 days. If fever persists beyond 3 days without improvement, contact your pediatrician for evaluation (Source: NIH | Common Infant and Newborn Problems: MedlinePlus – Fever (High Temperature) In Kids, Nemours Foundation).

Q: What home remedies help a child's fever?

Offer extra fluids, dress in light clothing, keep the room cool, and give fever-reducing medication (acetaminophen or ibuprofen) only if your child is uncomfortable—dose strictly by weight. Do not use cold baths or alcohol rubs (Source: NIH | Common Infant and Newborn Problems: MedlinePlus – Fever (High Temperature) In Kids, Nemours Foundation).

Q: Should I wake my child to give fever medicine?

No. If your child is sleeping comfortably, do not wake them for medication. Sleep supports recovery. Give medicine only if your child wakes up and is uncomfortable (Source: NIH | Common Infant and Newborn Problems: MedlinePlus – Fever (High Temperature) In Kids, Nemours Foundation).

Q: Can teething cause a fever?

No. Teething does not cause a true fever (above 100.4°F / 38°C). If your child has a fever, it is likely due to an infection, not teething. Contact your pediatrician if you are concerned (Source: NIH | Common Infant and Newborn Problems: MedlinePlus – Fever (High Temperature) In Kids, Nemours Foundation).

Q: What is yellow fever and should I worry in New York?

Yellow fever is a serious infection spread by mosquitoes that is not found in the United States, including New York. However, if you are traveling to parts of sub-Saharan Africa or South America, you should have a yellow fever vaccination and take precautions to avoid mosquito bites (Source: NHS | Yellow fever – Yellow fever).

Q: When should I go to the ER for my child's fever?

Go to the ER immediately if your child has a fever with difficulty breathing, a seizure, is difficult to wake, shows signs of dehydration, is under 3 months with any fever, or if the fever is above 104°F (40°C) and not responding to medication (Source: NIH | Common Infant and Newborn Problems: MedlinePlus – When to Seek Medical Attention, Mayo Foundation for Medical Education and Research).
