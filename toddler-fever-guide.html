Baby Fever in Children 0: Complete Parent Guide

Fever is one of the most common concerns for parents of young children. Understanding what a fever is, how to measure it accurately, when to treat it, and when to seek medical attention can help you confidently care for your child.

What Is a Fever?

A fever is a body temperature that is higher than normal . A fever is not a disease itself—it is a sign that the body's immune system is fighting an infection . When your child has a fever, their body is working to kill the virus or bacteria that caused the illness .

In children, a fever is generally defined as a rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher .

How to Measure Your Baby's Temperature Accurately

The method you use to take your child's temperature matters, especially in the first few months:

Rectal thermometer: The most accurate method, especially for infants under 3 months . The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends this as the gold standard for babies .

Armpit (axillary): Less reliable but can be used as a screening method for older babies .

Ear and forehead thermometers: Convenient for older infants and toddlers, but less precise for newborns .

Important: Do not use mercury thermometers due to the risk of breakage and toxic mercury exposure .

Normal Body Temperature Ranges

There is no single "normal" temperature for all children. Normal body temperature varies with age, activity, time of day, and measurement method :

Newborns (0Months)

A rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher in a newborn is a medical emergency . Call your pediatrician immediately or go to the emergency room without delay . Newborns have immature immune systems and can develop serious infections quickly.

Infants (3Months)

Fever up to 102°F (38.9°C): Have your child rest and drink fluids. Call your pediatrician if your child seems more irritable, has low energy, or is uncomfortable .

Fever above 102°F (38.9°C): Call your pediatrician, who may recommend bringing your child in for an exam .

Children (6Months)

For fever above 102°F (38.9°C), you can give acetaminophen (Tylenol) or ibuprofen (Advil, Motrin) if your child is uncomfortable . Do not wake your child to give fever medicine . Call your pediatrician if the fever does not respond to medication or lasts longer than one day .

Red Flags: When to Call the Doctor Immediately

You should contact your pediatrician or seek emergency care if your child with fever has any of these signs:

Age under 3 months with any fever — a medical emergency Fever lasting more than 3 days without improvement Febrile seizure — especially if it's the child's first seizure Lethargy or difficulty waking — the child hardly responds to you or is very sleepy A rash that does not fade when pressed — use a clear glass to check Difficulty breathing — rapid breathing, grunting, or crackling sounds Stiff neck — trouble moving the chin toward the chest Signs of severe dehydration — sunken eyes, very dry lips, no urination for 12 hours Repeated vomiting or diarrhea that cannot be managed at home Severe headache, earache, or abdominal pain Home Fever Management

When to Treat

Give fever-reducing medication only when your child is uncomfortable or in distress, not just because the number on the thermometer is high . The goal is to keep your child comfortable, not to achieve a "normal" temperature.

Medication Dosing

Acetaminophen (Tylenol): Give every 4hours as needed

Ibuprofen (Advil, Motrin): Give every 6hours as needed (for children 6 months and older) Always read the label carefully and use your child's weight to determine the correct dose. Never give aspirin to children or infants .

Comfort Measures

Keep the room at a comfortable temperature (not too hot)

Dress your child in light, breathable clothing

Offer extra fluids to prevent dehydration—water is best Use a cool cloth on the forehead for comfort

Do not use cold baths or alcohol rubs — these can cause shivering, which may raise body temperature further

The Bottom Line

Most fevers in children are caused by harmless viral infections and resolve within 1days . The number on the thermometer is less important than how your child looks and acts . Trust your intuition — if you are worried about your child, call your pediatrician .

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: When should I call the doctor for a fever in my child?

Call immediately if your child is under 3 months old with any fever, has a fever lasting more than 3 days, shows signs of dehydration, has difficulty breathing, has a seizure, or is unusually lethargic. If you are unsure, call your pediatrician for guidance .

Q: How long does a normal fever last in children?

Most viral fevers last 1 to 4 days. If the fever persists beyond 5 days without improvement, contact your pediatrician .

Q: Can a high fever cause brain damage?

No. Only body temperatures above 108°F (42°C) can cause brain damage, which is very rare from illness alone. Fever itself is not dangerous — it's the underlying condition that matters .

Q: Should I wake my child to give fever medicine?

No. If your child is sleeping comfortably, do not wake them to give medication. Sleep supports recovery. Only give fever medicine if your child wakes up and is uncomfortable .

Q: What home remedies help a child's fever?

Keep the room cool, dress your child in light clothing, and offer extra fluids. Use medication only if your child is uncomfortable. Do not use cold baths or alcohol rubs .

Q: Can teething cause a fever?

Teething does not cause a true fever (above 100.4°F / 38°C). If your child has a fever, it is likely due to an infection, not teething. Contact your pediatrician if you are concerned .

Q: When should I go to the ER for a fever?

Go to the ER if your child has difficulty breathing, a stiff neck, a rash that doesn't fade, a seizure, is difficult to wake, or has blue lips or tongue .
