Fever in Children: Understanding Fever and Accompanying Symptoms

Understanding Fever

A fever is a body temperature that is higher than normal. A normal temperature can vary from person to person, but it is usually around 98.6 °F (37 °C) (Source: NIH | Fever). It is important to understand that a fever is not a disease. It is usually a sign that your body is trying to fight an illness or infection (Source: NIH | Fever).

When your child has a fever, their body is working to kill the virus or bacteria that caused the infection. Most bacteria and viruses do well at normal body temperature, but when fever develops, it becomes harder for them to survive. Fever also activates your body's immune system (Source: NIH | Fever).

Common Causes of Fever

Infections cause most fevers (Source: NIH | Fever). These may include viral infections such as the flu, norovirus, or rotavirus—with rotavirus being the most common cause of acute diarrhea in children (Source: NIH | Diarrhea). Other causes of fevers include certain medicines (such as some antibiotics, blood pressure medicines, and anti-seizure medicines) as well as some childhood vaccines (Source: NIH | Fever).

Fever with Diarrhea

Diarrhea is defined as loose, watery stools. You have diarrhea if you have loose stools three or more times in one day (Source: NIH | Diarrhea). Acute diarrhea is a common problem that usually lasts about one or two days, though it may last longer before resolving on its own (Source: NIH | Diarrhea).

Diarrhea lasting more than a few days may be a sign of a more serious problem. Chronic diarrhea—defined as diarrhea that lasts at least four weeks—can be a symptom of a chronic disease. Chronic diarrhea symptoms may be continual, or they may come and go (Source: NIH | Diarrhea).

Weight considerations: Children with diarrhea lose fluids and electrolytes. The younger and smaller the child, the more quickly dehydration can develop. Infants under 3 months are particularly vulnerable.

Fever with Vomiting

Vomiting forces the contents of the stomach up through the food pipe and out of the mouth (Source: NIH | Vomiting). Common problems that may cause nausea and vomiting include infections of the stomach or bowels, such as the "stomach flu" (Source: NIH | Vomiting).

Nausea and vomiting may also be early warning signs of more serious medical problems, such as blockages (Source: NIH | Vomiting). Nausea is feeling an urge to vomit—it is often called "being sick to your stomach" (Source: NIH | Vomiting).

Weight considerations: Infants and toddlers have smaller fluid reserves. Persistent vomiting in a child under 2 can lead to rapid dehydration. A child's weight determines fluid requirements and the urgency of medical intervention.

When to Call the Doctor

Fever that is very high: Treatment depends on the cause of your fever. If the fever is very high, your health care provider should be consulted (Source: NIH | Fever).

Diarrhea lasting more than a few days: This may be a sign of a more serious problem (Source: NIH | Diarrhea). For children under 2, any diarrhea lasting more than 24 hours with fever warrants medical attention.

Vomiting as a warning sign: If your child cannot keep fluids down, or if vomiting is accompanied by severe pain, seek medical attention (Source: NIH | Vomiting). For infants and toddlers, inability to keep fluids down for more than 6 hours is a red flag.

Additional Signs Requiring Urgent Care

Seek immediate medical evaluation if your child's fever is accompanied by:

Signs of dehydration: Fewer wet diapers (less than 4 in 24 hours for infants), no tears when crying, sunken eyes, dry mouth, or lethargy

Severe pain: Such as severe abdominal pain (Source: NIH | Vomiting)

Difficulty breathing: Rapid breathing, wheezing, grunting, or visible chest retractions

A stiff neck: This could indicate meningitis

Confusion or unusual drowsiness: Difficulty waking or acting abnormally

A seizure

Green or bloody vomit or stool: This may indicate a blockage (Source: NIH | Vomiting)

Important for Infants

Any fever in a baby under 3 months of age requires immediate medical attention. Their immune systems are still developing, and what might be a minor infection in an older child can be serious in a newborn. Even without other symptoms, a fever in this age group should be evaluated by a health care provider right away.
