Vomiting in Children: A Guide for Parents in Chicago

Vomiting is a common symptom in children, often caused by a stomach virus (gastroenteritis) or an infection. Common health problems in babies include colds, coughs, fevers, and vomiting (Source: NIH | Common Infant and Newborn Problems: MedlinePlus – Common Infant and Newborn Problems). While most cases are mild and resolve on their own, it is important for parents to know the warning signs and how to care for a child at home.

Normal Vomiting and Home Care

For most mild cases of vomiting, home care is appropriate. The priority is to prevent dehydration.

Start with clear fluids: Offer small, frequent sips of an oral rehydration solution (ORS) like Pedialyte. For older children, diluted juice or a sports drink can be used, but ORS is best.

Wait before solid food: Do not give solid foods until your child has not vomited for several hours. If your child is hungry, start with bland foods like crackers, toast, or rice.

Continue breastfeeding or formula: For babies, continue to offer breast milk or formula, but in smaller, more frequent amounts.

Red Flags: When to Seek Immediate Care

You should contact a healthcare provider right away if your child has vomiting and any of these signs (Source: NIH | Gastroenteritis | Stomach Flu | MedlinePlus – Gastroenteritis):

Signs of dehydration: These include a dry mouth, no tears when crying, fewer wet diapers than usual (less than 4in 24 hours), sunken eyes, or a sunken soft spot on the head.

Lethargy: If your child is unusually sleepy, difficult to wake, or not interacting with you.

Severe or prolonged vomiting: Vomiting that is very frequent, or lasts more than a few hours.

Vomiting with a high fever.

Blood in vomit or stool.

Vomiting that is green, yellow-green, or looks like coffee grounds.

Severe abdominal pain.

Confusion or not responding as usual.

What is Cyclical Vomiting Syndrome?

Cyclical vomiting syndrome is a rare condition that causes severe episodes of feeling sick (nausea) and being sick (vomiting) (Source: NHS | Cyclical vomiting syndrome – Cyclical vomiting syndrome). Episodes usually start at the same time of day or night and last for the same length of time, followed by periods of feeling well (Source: NHS | Cyclical vomiting syndrome – Cyclical vomiting syndrome). The syndrome often starts in children around age 5 and improves as they get older (Source: NHS | Cyclical vomiting syndrome – Cyclical vomiting syndrome). If you notice your child has recurrent, patterned episodes of vomiting, seek medical advice.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: When should I call the doctor for my child's vomiting?

Call your pediatrician immediately if your child has signs of dehydration (dry mouth, no tears, fewer wet diapers), is lethargic, has severe vomiting, vomits blood or green fluid, or has a high fever (Source: NIH | Gastroenteritis | Stomach Flu | MedlinePlus – Gastroenteritis).

Q: How long does a stomach bug normally last?

Vomiting from gastroenteritis usually lasts 1 to 2 days. If vomiting persists for more than 2 days, contact your pediatrician.

Q: What home remedies help with vomiting?

The most important home remedy is to keep your child hydrated. Offer small, frequent sips of an oral rehydration solution (Pedialyte) or clear fluids. Once they have stopped vomiting for a few hours, you can offer bland foods.

Q: Does a stomach bug always include diarrhea?

Often, but not always. Viral gastroenteritis frequently causes both vomiting and diarrhea, but it can sometimes cause vomiting without diarrhea.

Q: Can I give my child medication to stop vomiting?

Do not give anti-nausea medication to a child without a doctor's guidance. These medications can have side effects and are not always safe for children. Focus on hydration, and consult a pediatrician if vomiting is severe.

Q: When should I go to the ER for vomiting?

Go to the ER if your child is difficult to wake, has severe dehydration signs, is vomiting green fluid or blood, has a seizure, or is having severe difficulty breathing.
