Preschooler Cough: When to Worry

A cough is a common symptom in preschoolers, and most of the time, it is caused by a viral infection that will resolve on its own. However, there are certain signs that indicate a cough may be more serious. If you notice any of the following warning signs, seek medical advice promptly.

Warning Signs: When to Call Your Healthcare Provider

You should contact your pediatrician or family doctor if your child&#x27;s cough is accompanied by any of these symptoms:

Fever: A persistent fever, especially one that lasts for more than 3days.

Rapid or Difficult Breathing: Your child is breathing faster than normal, or you can see their chest or ribs pulling in with each breath (retractions).

Wheezing: A high-pitched whistling sound when your child breathes out.

Signs of Dehydration: Your child is not drinking enough fluids, has a dry mouth, or is producing fewer wet diapers than usual.

Lethargy: Your child is unusually tired, drowsy, or irritable.

Cough Lasting More Than 3 Weeks: A persistent cough that does not improve.

Whooping Sound: A cough that ends with a &quot;whoop&quot; sound when the child breathes in.

Vomiting After Coughing: Coughing that is so severe it causes your child to vomit.

Whooping Cough: A Specific Concern

Whooping cough (pertussis) is a highly contagious respiratory infection that can be serious, especially in young children. The first signs of whooping cough are similar to a cold, such as a runny nose and sore throat (a high temperature is uncommon). After about a week, you or your child will get coughing bouts that last for a few minutes and are worse at night. You may also notice a &quot;whoop&quot; sound—a gasp for breath between coughs. The cough may last for several weeks or months.

If you suspect your child has whooping cough, seek medical attention immediately, especially if they are under 6 months old.

When to Seek Emergency Care (Call 112/999)

Call for emergency help immediately if your child has a cough and any of the following signs:

Difficulty breathing: Your child is struggling to breathe, gasping for air, or breathing very fast.

Blue or Pale Skin: Your child&#x27;s skin, lips, or tongue turn blue or pale.

Limp or Floppy: Your child is unresponsive, difficult to wake, or very drowsy.

Severe Choking or Gagging: Your child is choking or having trouble swallowing.
