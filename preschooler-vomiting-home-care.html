Preschooler Vomiting: Home Care and When to Seek Help

Vomiting in preschoolers is usually caused by a minor viral infection. The greatest risk is dehydration, which occurs when the body loses too much fluid.

Rehydration: The Top Priority

The primary goal is to replace lost fluids.

How to give fluids (avoid fruit juice or soda—too much sugar, lacks essential minerals):

Offer small amounts frequently (e.g., 1 teaspoon to 1 tablespoon every 5 minutes).

Start with clear fluids such as oral rehydration solution (Pedialyte), barley water, or rice water.

If tolerated for 4 hours without vomiting, gradually increase the amount.

For breastfed babies: nurse more frequently but for shorter periods.

Returning to solid foods:

After 8 hours without vomiting, offer bland foods like crackers, toast, bananas, rice, or soup.

Avoid high-fiber or high-sugar foods.

Resume normal diet within 24 hours after recovery.

Signs of Dehydration to Watch For

Contact your healthcare provider immediately if you notice:

No urine for 8 hours or fewer than 4 wet diapers in 24 hours.

Dry mouth, dry tongue, or no tears when crying.

Sunken eyes or cheeks.

Unusual sleepiness, irritability, or lethargy.

Cool, clammy skin or rapid breathing.

When to Seek Emergency Care (Call 112/999)

Get emergency care immediately if:

Vomiting contains blood or greenish material.

Projectile vomiting (forceful vomiting shooting out).

Severe abdominal pain or swelling.

Child is difficult to wake, confused, or extremely lethargic.

Signs of severe dehydration (sunken eyes, no urine for 12 hours).

Pale, mottled skin or feels abnormally cold.

Blood in stool or black, tarry stools.

When to Call the Doctor

Call your healthcare provider if:

Vomiting lasts more than 24 hours for children under age 2, or more than 48 hours for age 2 and older.

Child cannot keep any fluids down.

Vomiting with diarrhea.

Fever (especially if under 3 months with temperature 38°C/100.4°F or above).

Persistent abdominal pain or severe headache.
