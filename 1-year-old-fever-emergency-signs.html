Fever in a 1-Year-Old: Recognizing Emergency Signs

Fever is a common sign that your child&#x27;s body is fighting an infection, and most cases are harmless and resolve on their own. You can learn what the common warning signs are (Source: NIH | Toddler Health). However, it can be difficult to know when a fever signals something more serious. This guide will help you recognize the emergency signs that require immediate medical attention for your 1-year-old.

🚨 When to Go to the Emergency Room or Call 911

Call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department immediately if your child has a fever and any of the following:

Difficulty breathing: Look for fast, labored, or shallow breathing. If your child is grunting, sucking their stomach in under their ribs with each breath, or if their lips or face turn blue, this is a critical emergency.

A seizure (fit): This is a febrile seizure. Call 911 immediately if it is your child&#x27;s first seizure, if the seizure lasts longer than 5 minutes, or if your child has trouble breathing during it. During the seizure, place your child on their side on a soft surface (recovery position), and do not put anything in their mouth.

Extreme lethargy or unresponsiveness: If your child is very difficult to wake up, is unusually sleepy, confused, or unresponsive, get emergency help right away.

A non-blanching rash: A rash that does not fade or turn white when you press a glass against it. This can be a sign of a serious bacterial infection like meningitis.

A stiff neck: If your child resists moving their neck or cannot touch their chin to their chest.

Signs of severe dehydration: Sunken eyes, no tears when crying, a sunken soft spot (fontanelle) on the head, and no wet diapers for 8 to 12 hours.

🟡 When to Call Your Pediatrician Immediately

Contact your child&#x27;s doctor right away if the fever is accompanied by:

Age-specific temperature: For a 1-year-old, you should call the doctor if the fever is 39°C (102.2°F) or higher (Source: NIH | Fever Medical Encyclopedia) or if a fever of any temperature lasts for more than 3 days.

Persistent vomiting or diarrhea: Especially if your child is unable to keep fluids down.

Severe pain: Complaints of a severe headache, earache, or stomach pain.

Signs of dehydration: A dry mouth, cracked lips, no tears, and fewer wet diapers than usual.

Your child appears very ill: You know your child best. If they are inconsolable, have an unusually high-pitched cry, or seem &quot;off,&quot; it is always best to call your doctor for advice.

Trust your instincts. If you are worried about your child&#x27;s fever, contacting your pediatrician is always the safest choice (Source: NIH | Toddler Health).
