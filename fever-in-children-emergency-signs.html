Fever in Children: Emergency Signs Every Parent Should Know

Understanding Fever

A fever is a body temperature that is higher than normal. A normal temperature can vary from person to person, but it is usually around 98.6 °F (37 °C) (Source: NIH | Fever). It is important to understand that a fever is not a disease. It is usually a sign that your body is trying to fight an illness or infection (Source: NIH | Fever).

When your child has a fever, their body is actively working to kill the virus or bacteria that caused the infection. Most bacteria and viruses do well at normal body temperature, but when fever develops, it becomes harder for them to survive. Fever also activates your body's immune system (Source: NIH | Fever).

Common Causes of Fever

Infections cause most fevers (Source: NIH | Fever). These may include viral infections such as the flu, norovirus, or rotavirus—with rotavirus being the most common cause of acute diarrhea in children (Source: NIH | Diarrhea). Other causes of fevers include certain medicines (such as some antibiotics, blood pressure medicines, and anti-seizure medicines) as well as some childhood vaccines (Source: NIH | Fever).

Accompanying Symptoms: Diarrhea and Vomiting

Fever in children is frequently accompanied by other gastrointestinal symptoms. Diarrhea is defined as loose, watery stools. You have diarrhea if you have loose stools three or more times in one day. Acute diarrhea is a common problem that usually lasts about one or two days, though it may last longer before resolving on its own (Source: NIH | Diarrhea).

Vomiting is another common symptom—it forces the contents of the stomach up through the food pipe and out of the mouth. Nausea and vomiting can be caused by infections of the stomach or bowels, such as the "stomach flu" (Source: NIH | Vomiting).

Emergency Warning Signs

When fever is very high: Treatment depends on the cause of your fever. If the fever is very high, your health care provider should be consulted (Source: NIH | Fever). A very high fever, especially in young children, requires prompt medical evaluation.

When vomiting signals a serious problem: Nausea and vomiting may be early warning signs of more serious medical problems, such as blockages (Source: NIH | Vomiting). If your child cannot keep fluids down, this can lead to dehydration, which is a medical emergency.

When diarrhea persists: Diarrhea that lasts more than a few days may be a sign of a more serious problem. Chronic diarrhea—defined as diarrhea that lasts at least four weeks—can be a symptom of a chronic disease (Source: NIH | Diarrhea). While acute diarrhea typically resolves on its own, prolonged diarrhea with fever warrants medical attention.

Additional Emergency Signs Requiring Immediate Care

Seek emergency medical care immediately if your child's fever is accompanied by:

Signs of dehydration: Fewer wet diapers than usual, no tears when crying, sunken eyes, dry mouth, or lethargy

Severe pain: Such as with kidney stones (Source: NIH | Vomiting) or abdominal pain that is intense and persistent

Difficulty breathing: Rapid breathing, wheezing, or visible chest retractions

A stiff neck: This could indicate meningitis

Confusion or unusual drowsiness: Difficulty waking or acting abnormally

A seizure: Convulsions or uncontrolled movements

Green or bloody vomit or stool: This may indicate a blockage or other serious condition

Important for Infants

Any fever in a baby under 3 months of age requires immediate medical attention. Their immune systems are still developing, and what might be a minor infection in an older child can be serious in a newborn. Even without other symptoms, a fever in this age group should be evaluated by a health care provider right away.

When to Call Your Pediatrician

You should contact your pediatrician if:

Your child's fever is very high

Your child has diarrhea lasting more than a few days

Your child has persistent vomiting and cannot keep fluids down

Your child appears dehydrated

Your child has a fever that lasts more than 48 hours without an obvious cause
