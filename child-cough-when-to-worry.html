When Should You Worry About Child Cough? When to Call the Doctor

Coughing is common in children and is often caused by viral infections like the common cold. However, knowing when a cough is a sign of something more serious is essential for every parent. It can be scary when your baby or child is sick, especially when it is not an everyday problem like a cold (Source: NIH | Uncommon Infant and Newborn Problems: MedlinePlus – Uncommon Infant and Newborn Problems). If you have concerns about your child's health, call your health care provider right away (Source: NIH | Uncommon Infant and Newborn Problems: MedlinePlus – Sick Baby? When to Seek Medical Attention, Mayo Foundation for Medical Education and Research).

When Is a Cough Normal?

Most coughs in children are caused by viral respiratory infections and resolve on their own within 7 to 10 days. A cough that occurs with a runny nose, mild fever, and otherwise normal behavior is usually not a cause for concern. Children may cough more at night due to post-nasal drip, which is also common.

When Should You Worry? (Red Flags)

You should worry and seek medical attention immediately if your child has a cough along with any of these emergency signs:

Difficulty breathing: Fast breathing, flaring nostrils, grunting, or chest retractions (skin pulling in around the ribs or neck) require urgent care (Source: NIH | Uncommon Infant and Newborn Problems: MedlinePlus – Chest Pain in Infants and Children, American Academy of Family Physicians).

Chest pain with coughing (Source: NIH | Uncommon Infant and Newborn Problems: MedlinePlus – Chest Pain in Infants and Children, American Academy of Family Physicians).

Lethargy or difficulty waking: If your child is unusually difficult to wake, floppy, or not interacting with you, this is an emergency.

Poor feeding or signs of dehydration: If your child is not feeding or drinking well, has fewer wet diapers than usual, sunken fontanelle (soft spot in babies), dry mouth, or no tears when crying, these are warning signs (Source: NIH | Uncommon Infant and Newborn Problems: MedlinePlus – Feeding Problems in Infants and Children, American Academy of Family Physicians).

High fever (especially in a baby under 3 months old) or fever lasting more than 3 days.

Cough that lasts more than 10–14 days without improvement.

Cough that is worsening or comes in severe fits.

Blue or pale lips or face (cyanosis)—this is a medical emergency.

Stridor (a high-pitched sound when breathing in) or wheezing.

What to Do at Home

If your child has a mild cough and is otherwise comfortable, feeding well, and breathing normally, you can manage at home:

Offer extra fluids (breast milk, formula, or water for older children) to keep the throat moist.

Use a cool mist humidifier in your child's room to help soothe the airway.

For children over 1 year old, a small amount of honey (1/2 teaspoon) may help soothe a cough—never give honey to infants under 12 months due to botulism risk.

Elevate the head of the bed slightly for older children to help with post-nasal drip (never use pillows for infants under 1 year due to SIDS risk).

What to Avoid

Do not give over-the-counter cold or cough medicines to children under 6 years old without a pediatrician's approval—they are not recommended and can have serious side effects.

Do not use vapor rubs on children under 2 years old unless directed by a pediatrician.

When to Call the Doctor

Call your pediatrician if your child has a cough and:

The cough lasts more than 10–14 days

The cough is getting worse

Your child has a fever lasting more than 3 days

You are worried about your child's condition

Do not be afraid to ask questions about your child's care. By working together with your health care provider, you make sure that your child gets the best care possible (Source: NIH | Uncommon Infant and Newborn Problems: MedlinePlus – Uncommon Infant and Newborn Problems).

The Bottom Line

Most coughs in children are harmless and resolve with supportive care. However, knowing the warning signs and trusting your instincts is key. If something feels wrong, call your pediatrician—it is always better to check than to worry.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: When should I call the doctor for a child's cough?

Call your pediatrician immediately if your child has difficulty breathing, chest pain, lethargy, poor feeding, signs of dehydration, fever (especially under 3 months), cough lasting more than 10–14 days, worsening cough, or blue lips. If you are worried, call your health care provider right away (Source: NIH | Uncommon Infant and Newborn Problems: MedlinePlus – Sick Baby? When to Seek Medical Attention, Mayo Foundation for Medical Education and Research).

Q: How long does a child's cough normally last?

Most viral coughs in children last 7 to 10 days. If a cough persists beyond 10–14 days without improvement, contact your pediatrician for evaluation (Source: NIH | Uncommon Infant and Newborn Problems: MedlinePlus – Sick Baby? When to Seek Medical Attention, Mayo Foundation for Medical Education and Research).

Q: What home remedies help a child's cough?

Offer extra fluids, use a cool mist humidifier, and for children over 1 year old, a small amount of honey (1/2 teaspoon) may help soothe a cough. Never give honey to infants under 12 months. Avoid over-the-counter cough medicines in children under 6 years without a pediatrician's approval (Source: NIH | Uncommon Infant and Newborn Problems: MedlinePlus – Sick Baby? Q: When should I go to the ER for a child's cough?

Go to the ER immediately if your child has a cough with difficulty breathing (fast breathing, flaring nostrils, grunting, chest retractions), blue or pale lips, stridor (high-pitched breathing sound), severe chest pain, is difficult to wake, or has a high fever with any of these signs (Source: NIH | Uncommon Infant and Newborn Problems: MedlinePlus – Chest Pain in Infants and Children, American Academy of Family Physicians).

Q: Can teething cause a cough in babies?

No. Teething does not cause a cough. A cough in a baby is usually due to a viral infection or other respiratory issue. If your baby has a cough, contact your pediatrician for advice (Source: NIH | Uncommon Infant and Newborn Problems: MedlinePlus – Sick Baby? When to Seek Medical Attention, Mayo Foundation for Medical Education and Research).

Q: Is a cough worse at night in children?

Yes, coughs often worsen at night due to post-nasal drip from lying flat. Using a cool mist humidifier and elevating the head of the bed slightly (for older children) can help. Never use pillows for infants under 1 year due to SIDS risk (Source: NIH | Uncommon Infant and Newborn Problems: MedlinePlus – Sick Baby? When to Seek Medical Attention, Mayo Foundation for Medical Education and Research).

Q: Can I give my child cough medicine?

Do not give over-the-counter cold or cough medicines to children under 6 years old without a pediatrician's approval. These medicines are not recommended for young children and can cause serious side effects. Always consult your pediatrician before giving any medication to your child (Source: NIH | Uncommon Infant and Newborn Problems: MedlinePlus – Sick Baby?
