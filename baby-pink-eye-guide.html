Baby Pink Eye in Children 0: Complete Parent Guide

Pink eye (conjunctivitis) is one of the most common eye conditions in babies and young children. It can be alarming for parents, but the vast majority of cases are not serious and will resolve on their own. This guide will help you understand the causes, symptoms, and when to seek medical care.

What Is Pink Eye?

Pink eye is an inflammation of the conjunctiva—the thin, clear tissue that covers the white part of the eye and lines the inside of the eyelid. It can affect one or both eyes and is a common condition in young children.

Types of Pink Eye

1. Viral Conjunctivitis

Cause: Most commonly caused by adenoviruses, often associated with colds

Characteristics: Watery discharge, usually starts in one eye and may spread to the other

Treatment: Resolves on its own within 1weeks

Spread: Highly contagious

2. Bacterial Conjunctivitis

Cause: Bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae

Characteristics: Thick, yellow-green discharge that can cause eyelids to stick together, especially after sleep

Treatment: Often requires antibiotic eye drops

Spread: Contagious; requires careful hygiene to prevent spread

3. Allergic Conjunctivitis

Cause: Allergens like pollen, dust mites, pet dander

Characteristics: Itchy, watery eyes, often affects both eyes

Treatment: Antihistamine eye drops, avoiding allergens

Spread: Not contagious

4. Irritant Conjunctivitis

Cause: Shampoo, dust, chlorine, smoke

Characteristics: Temporary redness and irritation

Treatment: Usually resolves on its own within a day

Spread: Not contagious

Symptoms of Pink Eye in Children

Common Symptoms

Red or pink eye(s) – the white of the eye appears pink or red

Watery or thick discharge – can be clear, white, yellow, or green

Crusting – especially after sleeping, causing eyelids to stick together

Itching or irritation – more common with allergic conjunctivitis

Swollen eyelids – can occur in more severe cases

Sensitivity to light – a sign of more severe eye involvement

In Babies and Toddlers

Young children may not be able to express how they feel. Signs to look for include:

Rubbing the eye(s) frequently

Swollen or red eyelids

Fussiness or irritability

Eye discharge that is yellow or green

Crusting of the eyelashes

Home Care and Management

For Mild Cases (Without Red Flags)

Gently clean the eye: Use a clean, damp cloth or cotton ball, wiping from the inner corner outward. Use a fresh cloth for each eye to prevent cross-contamination.

Remove discharge: Clean the eye several times a day to prevent crusting.

Practice good hand hygiene: Wash hands frequently and avoid touching the eyes.

Avoid sharing towels, washcloths, or bedding with other family members.

Keep your child home from daycare or school until symptoms improve and a doctor advises it is safe.

What NOT to Do

Do not share eye drops or ointments with other family members.

Do not use over-the-counter eye drops without a pediatrician's guidance.

Do not use herbal or home remedies in the eye.

Do not use contact lenses (if applicable) until the infection has resolved.

Do not try to remove anything stuck in the eye yourself—seek medical help.

Red Flags: When to See a Doctor

You should consult your pediatrician if your child has pink eye and any of these signs:

Thick yellow-green discharge (likely bacterial)

Redness or swelling that does not improve within 24–48 hours

Eye pain or sensitivity to light

Fever (especially in a child under 3 months old)

Changes in vision or your child not tracking objects

Symptoms that worsen or do not improve with home care

Blurred vision or changes in vision

Signs of a lazy eye: shutting one eye or squinting when looking at things, eyes pointing in different directions, or tilting the head when looking at something (Source: NHS | Lazy eye – Lazy eye).

When Is Pink Eye an Emergency?

Seek emergency care immediately if your child has:

Severe eye pain

Sudden vision changes or loss of vision

Severe swelling of the eye or eyelid

A dark patch in the eye that's getting bigger (Source: NHS | Eye cancer – Eye cancer)

Partial or total loss of vision (Source: NHS | Eye cancer – Eye cancer)

A lump on the eyelid (Source: NHS | Eye cancer – Eye cancer)

Prevention

To reduce the spread of pink eye:

Wash hands frequently with soap and water

Avoid touching your eyes with unwashed hands

Do not share towels, washcloths, or eye makeup with others

Keep children home from daycare or school until a pediatrician advises it is safe

Clean surfaces that may be contaminated

The Bottom Line

Pink eye in children is common and usually not serious. Most cases resolve on their own with simple home care. However, certain signs require medical attention—especially in young babies or if there are concerns about vision changes or severe pain. If you are worried about your child's eyes, call your pediatrician—it is always better to check than to worry.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: When should I call the doctor for pink eye in my child?

Call your pediatrician immediately if your child has thick yellow-green discharge, severe redness, swelling, eye pain, fever, signs of vision changes, or if symptoms do not improve within 24–48 hours. Children under 3 months with any eye redness or discharge should be evaluated promptly.

Q: How long does pink eye last in children?

Viral pink eye typically lasts 7 to 10 days and resolves on its own. Bacterial pink eye usually improves within 24–48 hours of starting antibiotic eye drops. If symptoms worsen or do not improve, contact your pediatrician.

Q: What home remedies help a child's pink eye?

Gently clean the eye with a warm, damp, clean cloth—use a fresh cloth for each eye. Wash your hands frequently. Do not use over-the-counter eye drops without a pediatrician's approval (Source: NHS | Eye injuries – Eye injuries).

Q: Is pink eye contagious in children?

Yes, pink eye can be contagious, especially if caused by a bacterial or viral infection. Wash your hands frequently and avoid sharing towels, washcloths, or bedding with other family members. Keep your child home from daycare or school until a pediatrician advises it is safe.

Q: Can allergies cause pink eye in children?

Yes, allergies are a common cause of pink eye in children. Allergic pink eye is usually itchy, watery, and affects both eyes. It is not contagious. Your pediatrician may recommend antihistamine eye drops or oral antihistamines for relief.

Q: Can teething cause pink eye in children?

No, teething does not cause pink eye. Eye redness or discharge in a child is usually due to infection, irritation, or allergies—not teething. Contact your pediatrician if your child has eye symptoms (Source: NHS | Eye injuries – Eye injuries).

Q: When should I go to the ER for pink eye in my child?

Go to the ER immediately if your child has severe eye pain, sudden vision changes, severe swelling of the eye or eyelid, or a painful red eye with sensitivity to light. These signs may indicate a more serious eye infection or condition.
