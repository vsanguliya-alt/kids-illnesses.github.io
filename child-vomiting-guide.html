Baby Ear Infection in Children 0: Complete Parent Guide

Ear infections are one of the most common reasons parents bring their children to the doctor. They can be painful and distressing for both the child and the parents. While most ear infections are not serious and resolve on their own, knowing the signs, symptoms, and when to seek help is essential.

What Is an Ear Infection?

An ear infection, medically known as acute otitis media, is an infection of the middle ear, the air-filled space behind the eardrum. It occurs when fluid builds up in the middle ear, often after a cold or upper respiratory infection, creating a breeding ground for bacteria or viruses.

Types of Ear Infections

Acute Otitis Media (AOM): This is the most common type, characterized by a sudden onset of ear pain and fever, often with fluid buildup and inflammation.

Otitis Media with Effusion (OME): This is when fluid remains in the middle ear even after the infection has cleared, often without symptoms.

Chronic Otitis Media with Effusion (COME): When fluid remains in the middle ear for a long time or returns repeatedly, even without an active infection.

Glue Ear: This is a condition where the middle ear fills with a thick, sticky fluid, which can cause temporary hearing loss . A GP should be able to tell if it's glue ear by looking for fluid inside the ear.

Symptoms of an Ear Infection

The symptoms of an ear infection usually start quickly and include:

Ear pain (earache)

Discharge running out of the ear

A feeling of pressure or fullness inside the ear

Itching and irritation in and around the ear

Scaly skin in and around the ear

In Older Children (Toddlers and Preschoolers):

May complain of ear pain

May be irritable or fussy

May tug or pull at the ear

May have difficulty sleeping

May have a fever

In Babies and Young Children:

Young children and babies with an ear infection may also:

Be irritable or cry more than usual

Have difficulty feeding or refusing to eat

Tug or pull at the ear

Have a fever

Have difficulty sleeping

Be less responsive to sounds

Most ear infections clear up within 3 days , although sometimes symptoms can last up to a week.

Red Flags: When to Call the Doctor

You should contact your pediatrician immediately if your child has an ear infection and any of these signs:

Ear pain with a high temperature (fever)

Discharge coming from the ear (blood or pus)

Signs of a hearing problem, such as:

Being difficult to understand

Asking people to repeat what they say

Asking for the TV or music to be turned up loud

Struggling to hear people far away

Becoming easily distracted when people are talking

Finding it hard to concentrate or seeming tired and irritable because it's harder for them to listen

Ear pain with pain when swallowing

Sudden hearing loss after an ear infection, a very loud noise, or a change in air pressure (for example, from flying)

Sudden hearing loss along with dizziness, a spinning sensation (vertigo), or ringing in your ears (tinnitus)

Conditions That Can Mimic Ear Infections

Earwax build-up

An object stuck in the ear (do not try to remove it yourself – see a GP)

Perforated eardrum (particularly after a loud noise or accident)

Baby teething or dental abscess

Diagnosis and Treatment

A GP should be able to tell if it's glue ear by looking for fluid inside the ear. Antibiotics may be prescribed if glue ear causes an ear infection.

Your doctor will likely:

Ask about symptoms and medical history

Use an otoscope to look inside the ear

Check for fluid, redness, or signs of infection

May recommend a hearing test if needed

Treatment Options:

Observation and home care: Most ear infections resolve on their own without antibiotics

Pain relief: Acetaminophen or ibuprofen for fever and pain

Antibiotics: May be prescribed if the infection is bacterial, severe, or if the child is under 6 months old

Ear drops: For pain relief (prescription only)

Surgery: For recurring infections or chronic fluid buildup

Prevention

Keep your child's vaccinations up to date

Avoid exposure to secondhand smoke

Practice good hand hygiene

Breastfeed if possible

Avoid pacifier use after 6 months of age

When to Go to the ER

Go to the ER if your child has:

Severe, sudden ear pain

Signs of meningitis (stiff neck, high fever, sensitivity to light)

Sudden hearing loss or dizziness

A high fever with other serious symptoms

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: When should I call the doctor for my baby's ear infection?

Call your pediatrician if your baby has a fever, is tugging at their ear, is irritable, has trouble sleeping, or if you notice discharge coming from the ear. Call immediately if there are signs of hearing problems or sudden hearing loss.

Q: How long does an ear infection last in babies?

Most ear infections clear up within 3 days , although symptoms can sometimes last up to a week. If symptoms don't improve or worsen, contact your pediatrician.

Q: What home remedies help a baby's ear infection?

Acetaminophen or ibuprofen can help with pain and fever. Applying a warm (not hot) compress to the ear can provide comfort. Keep your baby upright to help with drainage. Never put anything in the ear without a doctor's guidance.

Q: Can teething cause an ear infection?

No, teething does not cause ear infections. However, teething can cause fussiness and irritability, and may be mistaken for an ear infection. If your child has a fever or is tugging at their ear, it is more likely an ear infection. Contact your pediatrician if you are concerned.

Q: Does an ear infection always need antibiotics?

No. Many ear infections are viral and will resolve on their own without antibiotics. Your pediatrician will recommend antibiotics if the infection is bacterial, severe, or if the child is under 6 months old.

Q: When should I go to the ER for an ear infection?

Go to the ER if your child has a severe earache, a high fever, is very lethargic, has a stiff neck, or has sudden hearing loss or dizziness. These can be signs of a more serious condition.

Q: Can ear infections cause hearing loss?

Temporary hearing loss can occur when fluid builds up in the middle ear (glue ear). This usually resolves as the fluid clears. If you notice ongoing hearing problems, contact your pediatrician for a hearing test.
