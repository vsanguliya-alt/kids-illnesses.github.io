Home Care for a Child Who Is Vomiting

Vomiting in children is most often caused by a viral infection that will resolve on its own without specific medical treatment. The primary concern during this time is preventing dehydration, which occurs when the body loses more fluid than it takes in. This article provides evidence-based guidance for managing vomiting at home.

Fluid Replacement: The Top Priority

The most important step is to ensure your child drinks enough fluids to replace what has been lost through vomiting.

Recommended Fluids by Age

Under 6 months: Continue offering breast milk or formula in small, frequent amounts. If not tolerated, consider a commercial oral rehydration solution.

6 months to 1 year: Offer undiluted breast milk or formula. If this is not tolerated, a commercial rehydration solution that contains sugars and salts is recommended. Do not use plain water as the primary fluid replacement at this age.

Over 1 year: Offer small amounts of clear fluids often. Options include:

Commercial oral rehydration solutions (e.g., Pedialyte) in liquid or popsicle formDiluted apple juice or sports drink (half water, half drink)How to Give Fluids

Give fluids in very small amounts (1 teaspoon to 1 tablespoon, or about 5ml) every 5 to 10 minutes. If the child vomits, wait 30minutes and then restart with even smaller amounts. The goal is to have them take in about 1 ounce (30 ml) per hour.

Important: Avoid giving plain water alone to babies under 1 year, and do not give your child over-the-counter anti-vomiting or anti-diarrhea medicines unless specifically prescribed by your pediatrician. Do not give aspirin to children.

Managing Diet

For the first 24 hours of an illness causing vomiting, it is best to keep your child off solid foods, as liquids are less likely to stimulate further vomiting.

Once your child has gone 8 hours without vomiting, you can slowly introduce bland foods such as crackers, rice, or soup.

If your child is hungry and asking for food, offer small amounts of bland food but avoid fatty, greasy, or spicy items for a few days.

Do not force your child to eat if they are not feeling up to it; focus on maintaining fluid intake.

When to Call a Doctor or Seek Emergency Care

Contact your child's healthcare provider immediately if you notice any signs of dehydration or if the vomiting is severe. Signs of dehydration include:

Decreased urination: Fewer than 6 wet diapers per day for infants, or no urine for 6hours for older childrenDry mouth, dry tongue, and few or no tears when cryingSunken eyesIn babies, a sunken "soft spot" on the headLethargy: Being very sleepy or difficult to wake up, or excessive fussinessSeek emergency care immediately if your child:
