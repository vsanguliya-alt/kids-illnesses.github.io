Sore Throat in Infants: Safe Comfort Measures

A sore throat can be distressing for an infant, making feeding and sleeping difficult. While most sore throats are caused by viral infections that resolve on their own, the most important step is to determine whether medical attention is needed, especially since bacterial infections can have serious complications.

Safe Home Care for Sore Throat

The sources provided do not list specific home remedies (such as honey or gargles) for infant sore throat. This is because honey is not safe for infants under 1 year old due to the risk of botulism, and many common remedies are not appropriate for babies.

The following measures can help comfort your infant:

Offer plenty of fluids – continue breastfeeding or formula feeding frequently. Fluids help keep the throat moist and prevent dehydration.

Use a cool-mist humidifier in your baby's room – moist air can soothe an irritated throat.

Keep your baby comfortable – hold your baby upright during feeding to help with swallowing.

Age-appropriate pain relief – if your baby is over 3 months old and uncomfortable, paracetamol (acetaminophen) or ibuprofen (over 6 months) may be used, dosed by weight (Source: NIH). Never give aspirin to an infant or child.

Rest – allow your baby to rest and sleep as much as possible.

When to Call Your Healthcare Provider

You should call your health care provider if your baby (Source: NIH):

Has a sore throat for more than two days

Has trouble or pain when swallowing

Feels very sick or very weak

Has a fever

Is not feeding well

Has a white or yellow coating on the tonsils

Has swollen glands in the neck (Source: NIH)

When to Seek Emergency Care

Get emergency care right away if your baby (Source: NIH):

Has a lot of trouble swallowing

Has difficulty breathing

Has signs of dehydration (fewer wet diapers, dry mouth, no tears)

Is limp, floppy, or difficult to wake

Has a rash that does not fade when pressed

Important Complications: Rheumatic Fever

Rheumatic fever is a very rare complication that can develop after a bacterial throat infection (Source: NHS). If you've had rheumatic fever once, it's more likely to come back, so make sure to get sore throats treated early (Source: NHS).

Symptoms of rheumatic fever usually appear 1 to 5 weeks after a bacterial throat infection and include (Source: NHS):

Pain and swelling in the joints (arthritis) – usually ankles, knees, wrists, or elbows

Pain in the chest, breathlessness, and a fast heart rate

Jerky, uncontrollable movements in the hands, feet, and face

Tiny bumps under the skin

Pale-red patches on the arms and tummy (abdomen)

If your baby has had a bacterial throat infection recently and develops any of these symptoms, seek medical attention immediately (Source: NHS).

Diagnosis

To diagnose tonsillitis, your child's health care provider will ask about symptoms and medical history. The provider will examine the throat and neck, checking for redness or white spots on the tonsils and swollen lymph nodes. Your child will likely have tests to check for strep throat, since it can cause tonsillitis and requires treatment (Source: NIH).
