Wheezing in Children: What Parents in Phoenix and Everywhere Should Know

Wheezing is a high-pitched whistling sound that occurs when a child breathes out. It can be a sign of narrowed or inflamed airways. Wheezing in children is common and can be caused by various conditions, including viral infections, asthma, or allergies. This article provides evidence-based information that applies to parents everywhere, including Phoenix.

What Is Wheezing?

Wheezing is a whistling sound that happens when air flows through narrowed or partially blocked airways. It is most noticeable when a child breathes out (exhales). Wheezing can be a symptom of:

Acute bronchitis – an inflammation of the airways that can cause wheezing, shortness of breath, a low fever, and chest tightness (Source: NIH | Acute Bronchitis | MedlinePlus – Acute Bronchitis)

Asthma – a chronic condition that causes the airways to become inflamed and narrow, leading to wheezing, coughing, and tightness in the chest (Source: NIH | Asthma in Children | Asthma Symptoms | MedlinePlus – Asthma in Children)

Viral infections – such as respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) or the common cold

Allergies – allergic reactions can cause airway narrowing and wheezing

Symptoms of Wheezing in Children

The symptoms of asthma in children include coughing (especially at night or early morning), breathing problems such as shortness of breath, rapid breathing, or gasping for air, dark circles under the eyes, wheezing (a whistling sound when they breathe out), and trouble eating or sucking in infants (Source: NIH | Asthma in Children | Asthma Symptoms | MedlinePlus – Asthma in Children). These symptoms can range from mild to severe (Source: NIH | Asthma in Children | Asthma Symptoms | MedlinePlus – Asthma in Children).

When to Seek Medical Attention

Contact your pediatrician or seek emergency care if your child has wheezing and any of these signs:

Trouble breathing – shortness of breath, rapid breathing, or gasping for air (Source: NIH | Asthma in Children | Asthma Symptoms | MedlinePlus – Asthma in Children)

Chest tightness or pain (Source: NIH | Acute Bronchitis | MedlinePlus – Acute Bronchitis)

Wheezing that is new, worsening, or not improving

Fever with wheezing

Difficulty eating or sucking in infants (Source: NIH | Asthma in Children | Asthma Symptoms | MedlinePlus – Asthma in Children)

Dark circles under the eyes (a sign of asthma) (Source: NIH | Asthma in Children | Asthma Symptoms | MedlinePlus – Asthma in Children)

When Is Wheezing an Emergency?

Seek emergency care immediately (call 911 or go to the ER) if your child has:

Severe breathing problems – shortness of breath, gasping, or inability to speak

Wheezing that is severe or accompanied by retractions (skin pulling in around the ribs or neck)

Blue or pale lips or face – a sign of low oxygen

Treatment for Wheezing

Treatment depends on the cause. Your pediatrician may recommend:

Inhaled medicines (bronchodilators) to open the airways if your child is wheezing (Source: NIH | Acute Bronchitis | MedlinePlus – Acute Bronchitis)

Asthma management – if asthma is diagnosed, your child may need daily controller medications and rescue inhalers

Avoiding triggers – such as allergens, smoke, or respiratory infections

If My Baby Is Wheezing, Could It Be Asthma?

Wheezing in babies and young children is often caused by viral infections, not asthma. However, if your child has frequent wheezing, especially with coughing at night or early morning, your pediatrician may evaluate for asthma (Source: NIH | Asthma in Children | Asthma Symptoms | MedlinePlus – Asthma in Children).

The Bottom Line

Wheezing in children is common and can be caused by infections, asthma, or allergies. If your child has wheezing with trouble breathing or chest tightness, seek emergency care immediately. For mild wheezing, contact your pediatrician for evaluation and treatment.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: When should I call the doctor for wheezing in my child?

Call your pediatrician if your child has new or worsening wheezing, trouble breathing, chest tightness, fever with wheezing, difficulty feeding, or if wheezing does not improve with home care. If your child has severe breathing problems, go to the ER immediately (Source: NIH | Asthma in Children | Asthma Symptoms | MedlinePlus – Asthma in Children).

Q: How long does wheezing normally last in children?

Wheezing from a viral infection may last several days to a week. If wheezing persists or worsens, contact your pediatrician. In asthma, wheezing may be chronic and require daily management (Source: NIH | Acute Bronchitis | MedlinePlus – Acute Bronchitis; NIH | Asthma in Children | Asthma Symptoms | MedlinePlus – Asthma in Children).

Q: What home remedies help with wheezing?

Home remedies include keeping your child calm, using a cool mist humidifier, and avoiding triggers like smoke or allergens. If your child has prescribed asthma medications, use them as directed. Do not use over-the-counter cough or cold medicines without your pediatrician's approval (Source: NIH | Acute Bronchitis | MedlinePlus – Acute Bronchitis).

Q: Can allergies cause wheezing in children?

Yes, allergies can cause airway inflammation and wheezing in children. If your child has wheezing triggered by allergens, your pediatrician may recommend allergy testing and management (Source: NIH | Asthma in Children | Asthma Symptoms | MedlinePlus – Asthma in Children).

Q: Is wheezing a sign of asthma in children?

Wheezing can be a sign of asthma, especially if it occurs with coughing at night or early morning, and if the child has other symptoms like breathing problems or chest tightness. Your pediatrician can evaluate your child for asthma (Source: NIH | Asthma in Children | Asthma Symptoms | MedlinePlus – Asthma in Children).

Q: When should I go to the ER for wheezing in my child?

Go to the ER immediately if your child has severe breathing problems, gasping, blue or pale lips or face, or if wheezing is accompanied by retractions (skin pulling in around the ribs). These are medical emergencies (Source: NIH | Asthma in Children | Asthma Symptoms | MedlinePlus – Asthma in Children).

Q: Can my child have wheezing without a fever?

Yes, wheezing can occur without a fever. Wheezing from asthma, allergies, or airway irritation may not be accompanied by a fever. If your child is wheezing and you are concerned, contact your pediatrician (Source: NIH | Asthma in Children | Asthma Symptoms | MedlinePlus – Asthma in Children).
